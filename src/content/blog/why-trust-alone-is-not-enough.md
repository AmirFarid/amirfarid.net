My lived experiences and the history of my home country, Iran, have shaped a set of values that I deeply believe in. I think that without these principles and the structures that protect them, individuals can easily become victims of other people's interests, ambitions, and desires, which do not necessarily align with their own.

I believe individuals should have the **freedom** to make **independent**, informed decisions without surrendering their **privacy** or blindly **trusting** powerful institutions. Technology should protect that **freedom** through **integrity**, **transparency**, and **independent verifiability**.

This statement is short, but it reflects the values that have shaped my interests and motivated me to do something meaningful to help achieve them in real life.

I think the first time I truly recognized the importance of these values was in high school, during a history class in which we were studying the coup of August 19, 1953, known in Iran as *28 Mordad*.

What stayed with me was the striking contrast between the slogans heard in the streets: people who appeared to be celebrating Mohammad Mossadegh with chants of "Long live Mossadegh!" before his overthrow, and crowds shouting "Death to Mossadegh!" as his government fell.

The [1953 Iranian coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953_Iranian_coup_d%27%C3%A9tat) was a complex event involving foreign intelligence agencies, domestic political groups, military intervention, and organized demonstrations. Propaganda and the manipulation of information through influential newspapers, rumors, and other communication channels played an important role in shaping public perception. Understanding people's beliefs, fears, and interests also made it possible to influence their behavior.

That was the moment I began to understand how easily people could be manipulated, especially when information was controlled by a limited number of actors and individuals lacked independent ways to verify what they were being told.

It was also when I started recognizing a recurring pattern in the history of the country where I was born.

I wish these examples were limited to Iran, but the problem is much broader and deeper. Throughout history, similar situations have occurred in different countries, institutions, and companies.

I am not particularly concerned here with whether the final outcomes of these events were good or bad. Some may have led to positive changes, while others had devastating consequences. My concern is the mechanisms behind them and how easily individuals can become vulnerable to manipulation.

Today, many governments and corporations possess the technological capabilities to collect information about individuals, understand their preferences, influence their decisions, and control the channels through which they receive information.

These capabilities can serve beneficial purposes, but they can also be abused.

**Our only defense against such actions, which can sometimes be deeply harmful, should not be trust alone.**

Consider a few examples from different parts of the world:

These events are different in their causes, circumstances, and consequences. However, they all raise questions about trust, information integrity, transparency, and the ability of individuals to independently verify what they are being told.

Such problems have motivated me to become deeply interested in these subjects, particularly **trust and trustworthiness**.

A significant part of my motivation for pursuing a Ph.D. comes from my desire to contribute to strengthening the security and trustworthiness of technologies and systems, making them more resistant to manipulation and abuse.

I want to contribute to developing technologies that protect **privacy**, preserve **integrity**, provide **transparency**, and enable **independent verifiability**, so that individuals can exercise their **freedom**, maintain their **independence and autonomy**, and make informed decisions without being forced to blindly **trust** those who control the systems they depend on.
